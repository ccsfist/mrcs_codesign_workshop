# 5. Community Exercises to Align AA and Adaptation Policy

**How do we bring the results of this feasibility analysis back to communities, and validate its assumptions?**

**This section presents a protocol for discussing climate risks and adaptation options with community members. It translates the Household Economy Analysis into simple, direct exercises in two-way communication.** 

For this field guide, we will use details and numbers from the example case discussed yesterday, but these details should be adapted to the context of the community being visited. For example, you may want to discuss pastoralism and rangeland management in some communities, and farming and agroforestry in others. Part 5.2 discusses this in more detail. 

The instructions below refer to "Game Sheets" which can be found in the Simulation panel of the Scoping Analysis Tool.

## 1. Risk Scenario Field Guide

**Overview**

This game is designed to illustrate how climate risk can constrain communities’ investment in productivity-improving adaptations, and how AA can alleviate that constraint, but only if communities understand its purpose and it is designed appropriately.

In this game, you are a farmer, pastoralist or fisher who is making input decisions for the upcoming season. You start the game with no savings. In Stage 1 of the game, before the planting season begins, you need to make three decisions: indicated by “Decision A,” “Decision B” and "Decision C" on the game sheet. For the first round of the game you will play Game Sheet 1, where you can decide what type of input to use ("Decision A"). For the remaining four or more rounds of the game you will play with Game Sheet 2, 3 and 4, where you have the additional options of putting money into savings or nature-based protection ("Decision B") and to participate in AA ("Decision C").

You make these decisions before you know whether the upcoming season will be "normal" or "bad". A normal season will have sufficient rainfall to support your crop growth. A bad season is one with low rainfall, causing drought. There is a 2-in-5 year risk of drought; in two out of every five years, you are likely to have a bad season. In order to explore all the possible outcomes, participants will play the game for several rounds. In each round you will play the same year; your score does not accumulate over rounds. The type of season is determined in Stage 2 of the game, after you have made your decisions in Stage 1\. In Stage 3 of the game, we determine your results, based on the decisions you made, and the type of season that occurred.

STAGE 1: MAKE DECISIONS  
*Decision A: Input Choice*  
First, you will decide on the type of seeds you would like to use for the upcoming season. You can choose between low quality input and high quality inputs. For farmers, high-quality inputs could mean climate-smart agriculture; for pastoralists, it could mean rangeland management, water tanks and fodder storage; for fishers, it could mean investing in mangrove restoration. The specific investments under consideration will depend on the community and the context. 

High quality inputs will increase your yields in a normal season, but also have a cost associated with them. This cost may not be a monetary payment; it could also represent foregone income due to the greater labor necessary for conservation activities. The yield for low quality inputs in a normal season is 100 units. The yield for high quality inputs in a normal season is 140 units. In a bad season, both types of inputs will yield 0 units.

Low quality inputs cost 0 units. High quality inputs need to be purchased or worked on. In either case, the cost of the inputs will be 20 units.

Directions: Choose one input type. Please circle the inputs of your choice – use low quality inputs at 0 units, OR pay 20 units and purchase high quality inputs.

*Decision B: Save Money?*   
The second choice you can make before the season starts is whether or not to set aside some of last season’s earnings for savings. If you do, your potential earnings are reduced by 10 units in a good year, but you are left with 10 units of income in the event of a bad year. You cannot save more money than 10 units, since most of your money at the end of the season has to go toward essential expenses. 

Directions: Choose whether or not to save some money. Please circle your choice – \[YES\], to participate, or \[NO\], do not participate

*Decision C: Participate in AA*  
The third choice you can make before the season starts is whether or not to participate in Red Cross's anticipatory action (AA) program. This program will provide a transfer in the case of a bad season (e.g. in a drought-prone community, when rainfall is low). There is no transfer in a normal season. 

If you decide (YES), to participate in AA, Red Cross pays 12 units in program cost. In the case of a bad season, the AA will give you an emergency assistance transfer of 30 units, reflecting what is feasible given Red Cross's AA budget. If you decide (NO) to participate in AA, you will not receive a transfer in the case of a bad year.

Directions: Choose whether or not to particpate in AA. Please circle your choice – \[YES\], to participate, or \[NO\], do not participate

STAGE 2: SEASON TYPE IS DETERMINED  
In your village/region, there is a 2-in-5 year risk of drought. This means that in one out of every five years, you are likely to have a bad season. In each round participants will play the same year, they are not playing consecutive years. After you have made your decisions in Stage 1, we determine whether this season will be normal or bad by making a draw. There are 2 possible outcomes from this draw \- a normal and a bad season. 3 of the packets of gum will be blue indicating a normal season. 2 of the packets of gum will be red indicating a bad season (i.e. a drought has occurred).

*Game Sheet 3 and 4*  
During later rounds of the game, if a bad season is drawn, we will make another draw from the hat whether or not there will be an AA transfer. Since there is a 1/5 chance that AA will not trigger in a bad season, 1 of the packets of gum will be red indicating a fail-to-act situation. 4 of the packets of gum will be blue indicating that the AA triggered as intended. 

Directions: Once the draw is complete, circle on your game sheet whether this season has been normal or bad, and whether (if applicable) you have received a payout or not. 

STAGE 3: DETERMINE RESULTS  
Now that your decisions have been made, and the type of season has been determined, you are ready to calculate your outcome for the planting season. Use the boxes at the bottom of the game sheet to calculate the Total Result. If the season was normal, fill in the Amount Made depending on which input choice you made. If the season was bad, your Amount Made will equal zero. If you decided to participate in savings, place the forewent income in the Savings Cost box.  If you decided to participate in AA, place the cost to Red Cross in the AA Cost box.  If the season was bad, then you will also place the transfer amount in the AA Value box. If the season was normal, your AA Value will equal zero. If you did not participate in AA, then both the AA Cost and AA Value will equal zero.

Directions: Fill in the boxes at the bottom of this worksheet. Calculate your total income for the season by adding the amount you made, your insurance payout and your savings value (if there is one) and subtracting the cost of the inputs, AA and savings (if you invested in improved inputs, participated in AA and participated in savings, respectively)

Facilitator Game Instructions

*List of suggested items*  
Documents:

* 4 game sheets (located at the end of the previous section)   
* facilitator instructions  
* participant instructions  
* result sheet  
* discussion points

Supplies:

* 5 packets of gum \- 2 packets of red gum (bad season) and 3 packets of blue gum (normal season)  
* opaque bag or container or hat  
* dice  
* markers  
* board or flip chart or larger piece of paper  
* pens and/or pencils  
* prizes (?)

*Preliminary preparations*  
In order to record each group's outcomes, the facilitator draws the following chart on the board (or flip chart, large piece of paper etc).

The facilitator should put 5 packets of gum in an opaque bag (or container, hat etc). 3 of the 5 packets of gum will be blue indicating a normal season and 2 of the 5 packets will be red indicating a bad season.

On the board (or flip chart, large piece of paper etc), the facilitator can write down some of the rules of the game

Before starting the game facilitator should go over the rules and briefly talk about each one

List of Rules:

* In each round you play the same year again to see the different possible outcomes  
* Drought occurs 2-in-5 years  
* Groups choose the type of inputs/whether to save/ whether to paticipate in AA before the rains come  

Game Instructions

*General Instructions*

The facilitator assigns participants in groups of 3-5 people. Each participant receives four game sheets (labeled Game Sheet 1,2,3 and 4\) and one participant instruction sheet. The facilitator informs participants that they will be reusing the same game sheets during the 4-6 rounds of the game. In Stage 1 of the game, participants will make decisions as a group. The facilitator assigns each group a number. In order to make sure that all participants understand the game, the facilitator should go over the game sheet and do a demonstration before groups make their choices.

First, the facilitator takes out all 5 packets of gum and demonstrates that one of the 5 packets is a different color indicating a bad season. The facilitator should inform participants that there is a 2-in-5 year risk of drought. This means that in two out of every five years, you are likely to have a bad season. In order to explore all the possible outcomes, participants will play the game for several rounds. In each round participants will play the same year, they are not playing consecutive years. If participants are unable to cover their expenses in one of the rounds, they can continue playing the game but they cannot win the game. 

The facilitator should go over the rules and read them out loud for the participants. Groups have 2-3 minutes to discuss and record their answers. After participants make their decisions in Stage 1, the facilitator makes a draw to determine whether this season will be normal or bad.

STAGE 1: MAKE DECISIONS

*Game Sheet 1 Instructions*  
Participants will play Game Sheet 1 for one round. Here, they can choose the type of input they would like to use in the upcoming season.

Decision A: Input Choice  
Using the Game Sheet 1, the facilitator points out that in Decision A, participants have two options \- (1) to use low quality inputs and (2) to inveset in high quality inputs for 20 units of money. The facilitator draws a packet of blue gum and asks participants: "How much would you have made in a normal season with \[for example\] traditional agriculture / climate-smart agriculture?" Next the facilitator draws a packet of red gum and asks participants about the outcomes in a bad season.

*Game Sheet 2 Instructions*  
Participants will play Game Sheet 2 for two rounds. This time they will have the option to save money as well. The facilitator should go over Decision B before participants start playing Game Sheet 2\.

Decision B: *Save Money / Cultivate Nature-Based Protection?*   
Using Game Sheet 2, the facilitator points out that communities could set aside some of last season's earnings for savings, in order to reduce their risk during a bad year. 

The facilitator will tell communities that if they participate in savings, their potential earnings are reduced by 10 units in a good year, but they are left with 10 units of income in the event of a bad year.

Now the facilitator will ask participants to make *two* choices in advance of the season \- (1) to invest in improved inputs or not and (2) to participate in savings or not. 

Decision C: Participate in Red Cross AA  
Using the Game Sheet 3, the facilitator points out that in Decision C, participants have two options \- (1) to participate in AA (2) to NOT participate in Red Cross AA. The facilitator then draws a packet of blue gum and asks participants: "If the season is normal, would you receive an AA transfer from Red Cross? If so, how much?" Next the facilitator pulls out a packet of red gum and asks participants the same question.

The facilitator should tell participants that AA is different from savings in that it costs more, and someone has to pay into it every year, regardless of whether the year is good or bad. However, it would cover more of their losses than savings during a bad year. 

If the participant choose in participate in AA, Red Cross will pay a program cost of 12 units in every year. If it turns out to be a bad year, the participate will receive an emergency transfer of 30 units. 

Now the facilitator will ask participants to make *three* choices in advance of the season \- (1) to invest in improved inputs or not, (2) to participate in savings or not, and (3) to participate in AA or not. 

*Game Sheet 3 Instructions*

In the following 1-2 rounds of the game, the participants will play with Game Sheet 4\. Inform participants that from now on there is 1/5 chance that AA will not trigger in a bad season. AA is not perfect and it does not always anticipate disasters. While you are explaining this to the participants, add this information to the list of rules:

- AA does not trigger every bad harvest year

Ask participants why they think AA might not be perfect, why there isn't a trigger every bad year. After you hear their answers explain that: "It is important to note that AA is based on predictions from scientific models, which are not perfect. If the prediction is incorrect, or is measuring the wrong thing, or if other hazards occur, AA will not be able to anticipate your losses." 

Emphasize that this does not happen often. Since there is a 2-in-5 chance of a bad year and a 1-in-5 chance that AA fails to trigger in a bad year, there is only a 2-in-25 chance that a farmer will experience a bad year which does not have a transfer.

Use Game Sheet 4 to show participants that in Decision C under the option to pariticpate in AA, it is now indicated that if there is a bad season the AA transfer is either 40 or 0\. If the facilitator draws a bad season, s/he will make a second draw from the hat, this time with 1 red token and 4 blue tokens. Since there is a 1/5 chance that AA will not pay trigger in a bad season, 1 of the packets of gum will be red indicating a failed trigger. 4 of the packets of gum will be blue indicating that the AA triggered as intended. 

STAGE 2: SEASON TYPE IS DETERMINED  
In Stage 2, after all groups have made their decisions, the facilitator (or one of the participants) makes a draw to determine the type of season. After each draw has been made, the facilitator should return the packet of gum back in the bag in order to ensure that the probabilities of having a bad year remain the same during each round of the game. The facilitator should make 5 draws in total. Each draw determines the type of season for the same year, each year you start with no savings.

Let participants pick from the bag and determine the type of season. Engage participants who look like they are not focused by making them draw the next season.

Hint: The facilitator should make sure that at least one of the 5 draws is a packet of red gum (bad season).

STAGE 3: DETERMINE RESULTS  
Each group will announce the choices that they have made and they will go over the results with the facilitator, who will record the answers on the board. The facilitator should go over the calculations with the groups. Below is an example of how the facilitator might do this:  
"Your group chose to invest in conservation agriculture and participate in AA, and the season turned out to be bad. Since the season was bad you made 0 units of money. You participated in AA, therefore you received a transfer of 30 units of money at the end of the season. After you pay your labor costs of 20 units of money, your group will have 10 units of money left. Red Cross paid 12 units of program costs this year."

When the facilitator records the outcomes in the chart, s/he can put the following symbols (T: traditional agriculture, C: conservation agriculture, S: savings, N: No savings, A: participate in AA, D: Do not particpate in AA.) next to the numeric outcomes. For example, if a group chooses conservation agriculture, savings and no AA, their response can be recorded in the table in the following way: 135CSD. Additionally, the facilitator can color code the years so that normal years are in blue and bad years are in red in the chart.  

If everyone makes the same selections, the facilitator goes over the remaining options in order to show the participants the range of possibilities. The facilitator can discuss the implications of the remaining outcomes in the following manner: "As you may see, the option to participate in AA with traditional agriculture was not selected by any of the groups. Let’s go over the calculation together so we can see the outcome in a normal year such as the one we have picked... [and then present the resulting calculations.]"

The facilitator should use the discussion questions and key points in the sections below. It is very important that the facilitator leads a discussion that summarizes the main points of the game. The facilitator can give participants a results sheet as well as a sheet with the main discussion points if they express interest.

Finally, consider how forecast information could fit into this scenario exercise structure. If communities have forecast information, they could update their decisions. There may be less need for financial protection from a bad year in this case. However, the efficacy of forecasts as a source of information depends on what communities’ prior expectations are, and on the accuracy of the forecast itself. 

## 2. Sample Prompts for Specific Livelihood Contexts

### 2.1 Agriculture and Agroforestry

Do you remember 2022? What happened that year to your crops, your income, your household? What did you have to do to get through it?  
If you had had fruit trees producing income that year, what would have been different?  
If you planted trees today and they took four years to produce, would you want them ready before the next year, like 2022?

### 2.2 Pastoralism and Rangeland Management

Do you remember 2009? What happened to your herd that year? How many seasons did it take to recover?  
In 2013, what was different about that year compared to 2009? What did your household do?  
If your community agreed to rest one grazing block this season, what would that cost you now and what would it give you in a year like 2009?


### 2.3 Fisheries and Mangroves

Do you remember 2019? What happened that year to your fishing, your gear, your income? When were you able to fish normally again?  
Did you notice changes in what you were catching later in that same year: different species, less catch? Why do you think that was?  
If mangroves take ten years to grow into a real buffer, when is the right time to start planting them?
